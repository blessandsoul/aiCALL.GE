<p><strong>TL;DR:</strong> aiCALL puts a Georgian-speaking voice agent on your restaurant phone. It answers every call during service and after closing, books tables against a live floor plan so it never double-seats, and logs each booking to your CRM.</p>

<h2 id="the-dinner-rush-problem">The dinner rush problem</h2>

<p>A restaurant phone rings hardest at the worst possible time. Friday at 8pm, the room is full, two servers are down, and the host is carrying plates. The phone rings four times and stops. That was a party of six who wanted Saturday night. They did not leave a message. They called the place down the street.</p>

<p>This is the quiet leak in most restaurants. The calls that matter most, the bookings, arrive exactly when no one can pick up. An <a href="/contact">AI voice agent</a> answers on the first ring while your team works the floor. It speaks calm Georgian, takes the reservation, and hangs up before the host has set down the plates. For the wider picture of how voice agents fit a hospitality business, see voice agents for clinics, restaurants, and hotels.</p>

<h2 id="a-day-in-the-life">A Saturday, call by call</h2>

<p>Picture one service through the agent's day. The hours are illustrative, but the pattern is real for a busy Tbilisi spot.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>11:40am.</strong> A caller wants a table for four at 1pm. The agent checks the floor plan, sees space, confirms the name, and books it. Total call: under a minute.</li>
  <li><strong>2:15pm.</strong> Someone asks if you have a terrace and whether dogs are allowed. The agent answers both from your knowledge base, then offers to hold a terrace table for Sunday.</li>
  <li><strong>8:05pm.</strong> Peak service. Three calls land in ten minutes. The agent takes all three at once: a birthday party of eight, a couple for 9:30, and a cancellation it removes from the book so the table reopens.</li>
  <li><strong>11:50pm.</strong> The kitchen is closed, the staff have gone home. A caller books a business lunch for Monday. A voicemail would have lost this. The agent captures it.</li>
</ul>

<p>By the next morning the manager opens the book and every reservation is there, with the caller name, party size, time, and any note. Nobody scribbled on a paper pad. Nothing got lost in the noise of a full room.</p>

<h2 id="how-the-booking-works">How the booking actually works</h2>

<p>The agent does not guess at your capacity. It reads a live source, your reservation system or a shared calendar, and only offers times that are open. When it confirms a table, it writes the booking back, so the next caller sees the updated availability. That guardrail is the whole point: a reservation line that double-seats is worse than no line at all. The booking mechanics are covered in <a href="/blog/voice-booking-no-double-book">availability-aware voice booking</a>.</p>

<p>It also handles the messy parts of a real reservation. A party that wants to change from six to eight. A caller who needs the high-chair noted. A no-show the host wants to record. Each of these is a small data write, and the agent does it cleanly, the same way a sharp host would, except it never forgets and never gets pulled away mid-sentence.</p>

<h2 id="more-than-bookings">More than a booking taker</h2>

<p>Most restaurant calls are not reservations at all. They are questions: are you open on the holiday, do you do takeaway, where do I park, is the set menu still on. A human spends real time on these, often during service. The agent answers them instantly from your knowledge base, in the caller's language, and only escalates the rare call that needs a person, a large private event, a complaint, a press query. For those it takes the details and routes them to your contact, so nothing high-value falls through.</p>

<p>Because the agent shares one brain with your other channels, the answers it gives on the phone match what your chatbot tells a customer on Instagram or WhatsApp. A guest who asks about the terrace by DM and later calls to book hears the same facts both times. That consistency is hard to keep with a rotating front-of-house team and trivial for a shared AI brain.</p>

<h2 id="languages-and-tone">Georgian first, but not only</h2>

<p>Tbilisi dining rooms serve a mixed crowd: locals, Russian-speaking regulars, tourists in English. The agent detects the caller's language and replies in it, and it can switch mid-call without making a thing of it. A French visitor who starts in halting Russian and gives up to English gets a smooth handoff, not a dead line. The Georgian itself sounds like a person, not a translation, which matters most for older callers who hang up on a robotic voice. More on the language engine is in the Georgian voice assistant for business.</p>

<h2 id="faq">FAQ</h2>

<h3 id="faq-1">Will it double-book a table?</h3>
<p>No. The agent reads your live reservation source and only offers times that are open, then writes the confirmed booking back so the next caller sees current availability.</p>

<h3 id="faq-2">Can it answer questions that are not reservations?</h3>
<p>Yes. It handles hours, parking, menu, and policy questions from your knowledge base, and routes rare calls like large private events to a person.</p>

<h3 id="faq-3">What happens to calls after closing?</h3>
<p>The agent answers around the clock and captures the booking or the lead, so a late-night caller becomes a reservation instead of a hang-up at your voicemail.</p>

<h3 id="faq-4">Does it speak Russian and English too?</h3>
<p>Yes. It detects the caller's language, replies in it, and can switch mid-call, which suits a dining room that serves locals and tourists.</p>
